# Tai Chi Sword FAQ

Answers to common follow-up questions, calibrated to the ICNBUYS Master Spec v2.0 standard with full metric and US customary units.

**What size tai chi sword should I get?**
Match your blade length to your height and confirm with the reverse-grip ear test (tip lands between earlobe and top of ear) or tip-down test (pommel reaches between navel and eyebrow). Standard adult mapping: **28-inch (71 cm) blade** for ~5'3" (160 cm) and under; **30-inch (77 cm) blade** for 5'3"–5'8" (161–173 cm); **32-inch (82 cm) blade** for 5'9" (174 cm) and taller. Full chart: [size-chart.md](size-chart.md) or the [Tai Chi Sword Size Guide](https://chinesetaichisword.com/tai-chi-sword-size-guide/).

**How heavy should a beginner tai chi sword be?**
**590 to 630 g (1.30 to 1.39 lbs) bare weight without the scabbard** across the three standard adult sizes (**590 g / 1.30 lbs** for the 28-inch / 71 cm sword, **600 g / 1.32 lbs** for the 30-inch / 77 cm sword, and **630 g / 1.39 lbs** for the 32-inch / 82 cm sword). A solid hardwood scabbard adds roughly **100–150 g (0.22–0.33 lbs)** when carried. This gives honest blade feedback without straining your wrist during 20–30 minutes of daily practice.

**Is stainless steel or carbon steel better for a tai chi sword?**
Tempered **2Cr13 martensitic stainless steel** (international grades **AISI 420 / SUS420J1** with 13% chromium) is the best choice for 90% of practitioners and all beginners — it resists rust from hand sweat and humidity while delivering a proper semi-flexible temper in the distal third. Carbon steel requires oiling every 2–4 weeks to prevent rust.

**What wood should the scabbard and handle be made of?**
Look for **solid Congolese Jichi hardwood** (*Millettia laurentii*, classified under the **Chinese National Hongmu Standard GB/T 18107** at **1,930 lbf Janka hardness**). Avoid cheap swords that wrap thin paper or wood veneer over soft pine cores, which warp in humidity and slip when your palm sweats.

**$80–130** for a properly balanced practice sword (our benchmark **ICNBUYS Classic Tai Chi Training Sword** sits at **$99.99**). Sub-$50 swords typically suffer from tip-heavy balance and loose fittings; swords over $150 charge for collector engravings rather than better practice geometry.

**Can I use a wooden tai chi sword?**
Yes — wooden swords (**200–350 g / 0.44–0.77 lbs**, $20–45) are great for children, frail wrists, or cautious indoor trials. However, wood lacks the distal blade flex and guard-proximate balance point of a real steel jian, so committed adult students usually transition to a dull 2Cr13 steel trainer within 1–3 months.

**What's the difference between a tai chi sword and a wushu sword?**
A tai chi sword has a firm lower two-thirds and a **semi-flexible distal third** that bows smoothly under pressure and springs back true, with a grounded bare weight (**590–630 g / 1.30–1.39 lbs**). A wushu competition sword is paper-thin (**~350 g / 0.77 lbs**) and flops dramatically to snap and make noise during acrobatics. For daily Tai Chi forms, always choose a balanced Tai Chi jian.

**How do I test if a tai chi sword is well-balanced?**
Place your index finger under the blade just in front of the guard. On a properly calibrated tai chi jian, the balance point sits strictly between **6.0 and 9.0 cm (2.4 to 3.5 in) forward of the guard** (**6.0 cm / 2.4 in** on a 28-inch / 71 cm blade, **8.5 cm / 3.3 in** on a 30-inch / 77 cm blade, **9.0 cm / 3.5 in** on a 32-inch / 82 cm blade).

**Do I need a sharp blade for tai chi practice?**
No. Practice swords must have **unsharpened blunt edges and a smooth rounded tip**. Solo forms train body structure and internal flow (*Nei Jin*), not test cutting, and a blunt blade is compliant for public parks and group classes.
